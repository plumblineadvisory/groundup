Who You Are as a Leader

Weekly Objective Understand what leadership actually means — not a title, not authority, but a way of showing up that changes what's possible for the people around you. Identify your natural leadership strengths, your honest blind spots, and the kind of leader you are becoming.

At your final capstone, you'll present all of it. Not as a school project. As the beginning of something real.

Key Concepts

Leadership is not a title — it's a decision You don't become a leader when someone promotes you. You become a leader the moment you decide to take responsibility for something beyond yourself — for a team, a customer, a community, or an idea. At The Paddock right now, you are already making leadership decisions: whether you speak up when something's wrong, whether you help a struggling coworker, whether you hold yourself to a standard even when no one's watching. Leadership is happening in you every shift. The question is whether you're doing it intentionally.
The difference between managing and leading A manager makes sure today runs smoothly. A leader makes sure tomorrow is worth showing up for. Both matter. But they require fundamentally different orientations. Managers think about tasks. Leaders think about people and possibility. In a small business — your business — you will have to do both. Understanding the difference helps you know which mode you're in at any given moment, and which one the situation actually needs from you.
Your history is not a disqualification — it's a credential The most common reason people in recovery hold back from stepping into leadership is the belief that their past disqualifies them. It doesn't. In many ways, what you've been through — and come through — gives you capabilities most leaders never develop: the ability to sit with someone in their worst moment, to understand that change is possible, to know from the inside what it takes to rebuild something from nothing. Those aren't weaknesses. They are leadership assets of the highest order.
Integrity is the foundation every other leadership quality rests on You can be charismatic without integrity. You can be intelligent without it. You can be likable without it. But you cannot be trusted without it. And trust is the currency that makes leadership possible. Integrity means doing what you said you would do, when you said you'd do it, even when no one is checking. It means your words and your actions are the same thing. In a recovery context, integrity is something you've been practicing every day. It turns out that's exactly what leadership requires.
The best leaders are the best learners Research on effective leadership consistently finds that the quality that most predicts long-term leadership success is not intelligence, charisma, or experience — it's coachability. The willingness to receive feedback without becoming defensive, to admit what you don't know, to try something differently when it isn't working. Completing this program is evidence that you have that quality. You showed up, you did the work, and you were willing to learn things that were sometimes uncomfortable. That is leadership behavior.

Session 4 · Week 2

Vision, Mission & Your Why

Weekly Objective Write your personal vision statement — where you're going — and your personal mission statement — how you operate right now. Then go deeper: understand the "why" behind your business that will sustain you through the hard days when the money isn't there yet and the doubt is loud.
☕

Key Concepts

Vision is where you're going — mission is how you operate today A vision statement describes the future you're working toward — the best version of your professional life, specific enough to make decisions against. A mission statement describes what you do, how you do it, and why — right now, today, in your actual work. The distinction matters. Vision without mission is daydreaming. Mission without vision is treadmill running. You need both: a destination and a way of traveling that you're proud of.
Your vision doesn't have to be grand — it has to be yours The word "vision" can feel intimidating — like it requires a world-changing statement. It doesn't. A vision that says "I want to be running a thriving catering business in Shelbyville within three years, employing two people from the community, and being known as someone who kept her word" is a powerful vision. It's specific. It's real. It belongs to one person. A vision that sounds impressive but doesn't actually belong to you will not sustain you through the hard days. Only the true one will.
Start with Why — Simon Sinek's most important idea Simon Sinek's research found that the most successful businesses and leaders don't lead with what they do or how they do it — they lead with why they do it. The why is the belief, the purpose, the cause that gets you out of bed on a Tuesday when nothing is going right. For a business built after a period of personal rebuilding, the why is often the most compelling thing about it. People don't buy what you do. They buy why you do it. Do you know yours?
Your mission statement is short enough to memorize and true enough to live by The best personal mission statements are one or two sentences. They describe how you show up — not what you aspire to someday, but what you commit to right now. "I show up fully, treat every person I serve as if they matter, and leave every situation better than I found it" is a mission statement. You could say it before every shift and mean every word. If you can't memorize yours, it's too long. If you can memorize it but don't believe it, it's not true enough yet.
Clear values make hard decisions easy Every business eventually faces a moment when the profitable choice and the right choice are not the same thing. The businesses that stay intact through those moments are the ones whose owners know their values before the pressure arrives. Values aren't a list you put on a website — they're the things you'd sacrifice money or convenience to protect. Knowing what you will not compromise, in advance, is one of the most practical leadership decisions you can make.

Session 4 · Week 3

Pitch Your Business

Weekly Objective Learn how to pitch your business in two minutes — clearly, confidently, and in a way that makes someone want to know more. Practice the pitch until it feels natural. Then learn what comes after the pitch: how to handle questions, objections, and the most important conversation in any business — the one where someone says yes.
☕

Key Concepts

A pitch is a test of your own clarity — not a performance If you can't explain your business idea clearly in two minutes, you don't understand it well enough yet. That's not a criticism — it's useful information. The process of building a pitch forces you to answer the five questions every business idea must be able to answer: What is it? Who is it for? What problem does it solve? How does it make money? Why are you the right person to build it? If any of those five are fuzzy when you say them out loud, that's where more thinking needs to happen.
The two-minute pitch structure The most effective short business pitches follow a simple structure: Hook (one sentence that makes the listener want to hear more), Problem (the gap you're filling), Solution (what you offer and how it works), Customer (exactly who you serve), Business model (how money comes in), and Ask or Close (what you want from this conversation — a customer, a referral, an investment, feedback). Two minutes feels short. It is enough, if every sentence earns its place.
People fund people — not business plans Research on investor and customer decision-making consistently shows that the person behind the business matters more than almost any other factor — especially in the early stages. Your credibility, your conviction, your willingness to be honest about what you don't know, your track record of following through — these are what actually drive decisions. The business plan is the evidence. You are the reason someone would believe in it.
Handle objections with curiosity, not defensiveness When someone pushes back on your pitch — "the market is too small," "how will you compete with X," "the margins seem thin" — the instinct is to defend. The better response is to get curious. "That's a fair concern — here's how I think about it." Or even: "I'm not sure yet — here's how I plan to find out." Investors and customers trust a founder who can hold a challenge without collapsing or doubling down. Curiosity signals confidence. Defensiveness signals fragility.
Practice until the pitch disappears into conversation The goal of practicing a pitch is not to memorize a script — it's to internalize the story so thoroughly that you can tell it in any order, at any length, to any audience, without notes. A pitch that sounds memorized creates distance. A pitch that sounds like a natural conversation creates connection. The only way to get from script to conversation is repetition. Say it to your Mentor Lead, to a friend, to a mirror, to yourself in the car. Say it until you believe every word of it.

Your 90-Day Launch Plan

Weekly Objective Build a concrete, specific, accountable plan for the 90 days after this program ends. Not a wish list — a plan. Specific actions, real deadlines, and someone who will hold you to them. This is the bridge between what you've built in this program and the life you're building after it.
☕

Key Concepts

The first 90 days after a program are the most important Research on behavior change consistently shows that the period immediately following a structured program is the highest-risk moment for momentum loss. The structure disappears. The weekly check-ins stop. The accountability fades. And the habits that were built in a supported environment have to survive without the support. The 90-day plan exists to bridge that gap — to create enough specific, accountable structure in the months immediately after this program that the momentum carries rather than collapses.
Specific beats ambitious every time "I'm going to grow my business" is not a plan. "I'm going to reach out to five potential catering clients by the end of this month, follow up with each one within a week, and land my first booking before Day 60" is a plan. The difference is that the second version has actions, timelines, and a way to know whether it happened. Ambitious goals without specific actions are the most common way people fail to follow through on things they genuinely meant to do.
Accountability is the missing ingredient in most plans Studies show that people are 65% more likely to complete a goal if they commit it to someone else — and 95% more likely if they have a specific accountability appointment to check in. Your Mentor Lead doesn't disappear after this program ends. Your Sponsor is still there. Awake Ministries is still there. The question is whether you build accountability into your 90-day plan deliberately — or leave it to chance and good intentions.
Start before you're ready The most common reason new entrepreneurs delay launching is waiting until everything is perfect — the logo, the website, the product, the pricing, the plan. Nothing is ever perfect before it launches. The businesses that survive are the ones that launched imperfect and improved. Your first customer will not care whether your logo is final. They will care whether what you delivered was worth paying for. Start with what you have. Improve with what you learn.
What you've built here is real Over four sessions you have built a Personal Brand Statement, a Business Budget, a Marketing Plan, and a Leadership Identity. You have studied customer service, finance, marketing, and leadership through the lens of a working café. You have developed AI skills you will use for the rest of your career. You have written a business plan that belongs to you and nobody can take. That is not nothing. That is a foundation. What happens next depends on what you do with it.
