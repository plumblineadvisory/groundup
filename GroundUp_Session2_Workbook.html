How Money Works in a Café

Weekly Objective Understand where The Paddock's money comes from, where it goes, and what's left over. Learn the three numbers that determine whether any business — café or otherwise — survives: revenue, costs, and profit. Begin applying this lens to your own business idea.
🗂 Ground Up Plan — Session 2

This Month You're Building: Your Business Budget

By the end of this session you'll have a real financial model for your business idea — what it costs to run, how you'd price your product or service, and the number of sales you need each week to break even. This is Ground Up Plan Deliverable #2. The numbers don't have to be perfect. They have to be honest.

☕

Key Concepts

Revenue, costs, and profit — the three numbers every business lives by Revenue is every dollar that comes in. Costs are everything you spend to run the business. Profit is what's left over after you subtract costs from revenue. A café can be packed every morning and still lose money — if its costs are higher than its revenue. Understanding this relationship is the foundation of every financial decision a business ever makes. It sounds simple. Most businesses that fail missed it.
Fixed costs vs. variable costs Fixed costs are what you pay whether you sell one cup of coffee or one thousand — rent, insurance, equipment loans. Variable costs change with how much you sell — coffee beans, milk, cups, the extra labor during a rush. Knowing which costs are fixed and which are variable helps you understand where your financial risk lives and what you can actually control. You can't negotiate rent away. You can optimize how much milk your baristas steam.
What The Paddock's money looks like A typical independent café like The Paddock earns revenue from coffee drinks, food, baked goods, and catering. Food and beverage costs (what the ingredients cost) typically run 25–35% of revenue. Labor is the biggest expense — often 30–35% of revenue. Add rent, utilities, supplies, and insurance, and a well-run independent café typically keeps 5–15% of revenue as profit. That means for every $10 latte sold, $0.50 to $1.50 is actual profit. The rest runs the business.
Cash flow is not the same as profit Profit is an accounting concept. Cash flow is what's in the bank right now. A business can be profitable on paper and still run out of cash — if customers owe it money it hasn't collected yet, or if it paid for supplies before it sold them. More small businesses fail from cash flow problems than from lack of profit. Understanding the difference between "we made money this month" and "we have money right now" is one of the most important financial distinctions you can learn.
Smaller is easier to start — and easier to learn from A full coffee shop startup costs $80,000–$300,000. A coffee cart or pop-up can launch for $5,000–$20,000. A catering side hustle or home baking operation can start for a few hundred dollars. The financial principles are identical at every scale — the math just has fewer zeros. Starting small isn't settling. It's the most financially intelligent way to test whether your business idea actually works before betting everything on it.
82%
of small businesses that fail cite cash flow problems as the cause — not lack of customers or bad products.
2.5%

The Basic Business Equation

Revenue − Costs = Profit (or Loss)
Example at The Paddock:
A busy Saturday: 150 customers, average ticket $8.50
Revenue: 150 × $8.50 = $1,275
Food & beverage costs (~28%): $357
Labor (2 staff × 8 hrs × $14/hr): $224
Share of daily fixed costs (rent, utilities, etc.): $180
Profit that day: $1,275 − $761 = $514
This is a simplified example — real P&Ls include many more line items.

Food Cost & Pricing Your Idea

Weekly Objective Understand how The Paddock prices its menu using food cost percentage — and apply the same pricing logic to your business idea. Learn why price is not just about what you charge, but about what it costs you to deliver and what the market will actually pay.
☕

Key Concepts

Food cost percentage — the most important number in a café Food cost percentage is what your ingredients cost as a percentage of what you charge for the item. For a café, the target is typically 25–35%. That means if a latte costs you $1.50 in ingredients (coffee, milk, cup, lid), you should charge $4.50–$6.00 to hit your target. If you charge $3.00, you're building a money-losing business one cup at a time — even if the café is full.
The three pricing questions every business must answer What does it cost me to produce? What will the market pay? What do I need to charge to be profitable? All three answers have to be true at the same time. A price that doesn't cover your costs makes you go broke slowly. A price the market won't pay makes you go broke fast. A price that isn't profitable enough to sustain the business makes you go broke eventually. Pricing is not about what feels fair — it's about what makes the math work.
Your time is a cost — most new entrepreneurs forget this When you're building a business, you will be tempted to not pay yourself — to just reinvest everything. That's sometimes necessary early on, but your time has to be included in your cost calculation. If you make 50 cupcakes in four hours to sell at a farmers market, those four hours are a cost. Divide the cost of your time into your pricing or you're not actually running a business — you're running an expensive hobby.
Price signals value — don't underprice yourself out of credibility One of the most common mistakes first-time entrepreneurs make is pricing too low. They think low prices attract more customers. Sometimes they do. But they also signal low quality. And they make it nearly impossible to raise prices later without losing customers. Research on consumer psychology consistently shows that people use price as a proxy for quality, especially when they don't know you yet. Price what your product is worth.
Shelbyville pricing is different from Louisville or Lexington Location affects what prices the market will accept. A 12oz latte in downtown Louisville might go for $7.00. The same drink in Shelbyville might top out at $5.50–$6.00. This isn't a problem — it also means your rent, labor costs, and other overhead are significantly lower. A café in a smaller market with lower overhead can actually have better margins than a city location with a higher price point. Know your market before you set your price.
25–35%
target food cost percentage for a healthy café — meaning ingredients should cost no more than 35 cents of every dollar you charge.
60%
of new entrepreneurs underprice their first product or service — often costing themselves more than any other single business mistake.

The Pricing Formula

Price = Ingredient Cost ÷ Target Food Cost %
Example — Latte at The Paddock:
Ingredient cost (espresso + milk + cup + lid): $1.65
Target food cost: 30%
Minimum price: $1.65 ÷ 0.30 = $5.50

Example — Your business (baked goods):
Ingredient cost per dozen cookies: $4.50
Your time (1 hour @ $15/hr): $15.00
Total cost per dozen: $19.50
Target food cost: 35%
Minimum price per dozen: $19.50 ÷ 0.35 = $55.71
If $55 per dozen feels too high for your market, your ingredient or time costs need to come down — not your price.

Labor, Time & Your Personal Financial Foundation

Weekly Objective Understand why labor is every café's biggest expense — and how to budget your own time as a business asset. Then zoom out from the business to your personal finances: learn the 50/30/20 rule and build a simple personal budget that supports your life in recovery and your business goals.
☕

Key Concepts

Labor is the largest cost in any service business At The Paddock — and at most cafés — labor typically runs 30–35% of revenue. That means for every dollar that comes in, about 33 cents pays the people making the coffee, serving the food, and keeping the place running. When a café has a slow Tuesday, the espresso machine costs the same whether it runs all day or sits idle. The staff still need to be paid. This is why scheduling and labor efficiency are so critical — and why understanding labor cost makes you a more valuable employee right now, today.
Your time budget and your money budget are the same thing Every hour you work is an hour you're not doing something else. When you own a business, especially early on, you will wear every hat — barista, accountant, marketer, delivery driver, customer service. Understanding where your time goes is as important as understanding where your money goes. Many entrepreneurs work 60 hours a week for a business that pays them less than a part-time job would. Track your time like you track your money.
The 50/30/20 rule — a simple personal budget framework One of the most practical personal finance frameworks: spend 50% of your take-home pay on needs (rent, utilities, food, transportation), 30% on wants (anything that makes life enjoyable), and save or pay down debt with 20%. In a recovery context, financial instability is one of the biggest stressors that can threaten sobriety. A simple budget isn't just a money tool — it's a stability tool. Knowing where your money is going removes one of the most anxiety-producing unknowns in daily life.
Separate your business money from your personal money — from day one The single most common financial mistake new business owners make is mixing personal and business funds. It makes taxes a nightmare, makes it impossible to know if the business is actually profitable, and tends to lead to "borrowing" from the business for personal expenses that never get paid back. Even if your business starts small, open a separate account for it. Treat every business dollar as belonging to the business — not to you personally.
Financial stability is part of recovery — not separate from it Research consistently shows that financial stress is one of the top relapse triggers. Not because money is everything, but because financial chaos creates the kind of sustained anxiety that recovery requires peace from. Building a simple, honest personal budget — and sticking to it — is one of the most concrete things you can do to protect your sobriety and build the foundation your business will need to stand on.

Reading the Numbers — P&L and Break-Even

Weekly Objective Learn to read a Profit & Loss statement — the report that tells you whether a business is making or losing money. Calculate your business idea's break-even point: the number of sales you need to make before you start earning real profit. Assemble your complete Business Budget for the capstone.
☕

Key Concepts

The P&L — a business's most important report card A Profit and Loss statement (P&L) shows revenue, costs, and profit over a specific period — usually a month. It answers the most important question in any business: are we making money? A P&L has three main sections: Revenue (all income), Cost of Goods Sold (what it cost to produce what you sold), and Operating Expenses (everything else — rent, utilities, labor, insurance). Gross profit is revenue minus COGS. Net profit is gross profit minus operating expenses. Net profit is what matters.
Break-even: the most important number for a new business Your break-even point is the number of sales you need to make before you start actually earning profit. Below break-even, every sale moves you toward zero. Above it, every sale is profit. Every new business has a break-even point, and knowing yours before you open is the difference between going in with a plan and hoping for the best. A coffee cart that costs $800/month to operate and earns $5 profit per drink needs to sell 160 drinks per month just to break even — about 6 per day.
Startup costs vs. ongoing costs Startup costs are the one-time expenses of launching — equipment, licenses, initial supplies, branding. Ongoing costs are what it costs every month to operate. Both need to be in your business budget. Many first-time entrepreneurs budget carefully for startup costs and underestimate ongoing costs — then run out of money before the business has had time to prove itself. The rule of thumb: plan for three to six months of operating expenses as a cash reserve before you launch.
Your Business Budget is a living document — not a prediction The Business Budget you build this month will be wrong. Not because you did it badly, but because all budgets are educated guesses until reality arrives. What matters is not that the numbers are perfectly accurate — it's that you have a plan you can measure against reality, update when things change, and use to make better decisions. A budget you update every month is worth a hundred times more than a perfect plan you never revisit.
3–6
months of operating expenses in reserve — the minimum cash cushion every new business needs before it's ready to launch.
18 mo.
average time for a new café to reach break-even — which is why starting lean and building slowly is smarter than betting everything on a big launch.

Break-Even Calculation

Break-Even Units = Monthly Fixed Costs ÷ (Price − Variable Cost Per Unit)
Example — Small baked goods business:
Monthly fixed costs (phone, supplies, market booth fee): $250
Price per dozen cookies: $18
Variable cost per dozen (ingredients): $5
Contribution per dozen: $18 − $5 = $13
Break-even: $250 ÷ $13 = 19.2 dozens per month (about 5 per week)

Every dozen sold above 20/month is pure profit. Does selling 20 dozens a month feel achievable? That's the break-even reality check.

What Makes a Business Budget Credible

Anyone can make up numbers that look good on paper. What makes a budget credible is the thinking behind it — being able to explain where each number came from, what assumptions you made, and what you'd do if those assumptions turn out to be wrong. Your Mentor Lead and the leadership team won't be impressed by optimistic projections. They'll be impressed by honest math and clear thinking.
